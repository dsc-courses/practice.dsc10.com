The Oscars, or Academy Awards, are the highest awards in the film
industry, awarded each year to the best movies of that year. The
`oscars` DataFrame contains a row for each movie that has ever been
nominated for an Oscar. The `"name"` column contains the name of the
movie and the `"rating"` column contains a rating of the movie on a 0 to
100 scale. This number incorporates many factors, but we won't worry
about how it is computed.

# BEGIN SUBPROB

Fill in the blanks below to collect a **simple random sample** of 400
movies from the `oscars` DataFrame, then calculate 10,000 bootstrapped
sample mean ratings.

    my_sample = __(x)__
    n_resamples = 10000
    boot_means = np.array([])
    for i in np.arange(n_resamples):
        resample = __(y)__
        mean = __(z)__
        boot_means = np.append(boot_means, mean)


# BEGIN SOLUTION

**Answer (x):** `oscars.sample(400)`

`.sample(400)` randomly selects 400 rows from `oscars`. By default, `.sample` samples **without** replacement, so no movie can be picked twice, which is exactly what a simple random sample is.

<average>85</average>

**Answer (y):** `my_sample.sample(400, replace=True)`

To bootstrap, we resample from our **sample**, `my_sample`, not from the population `oscars`. Bootstrapping is meant for when we only have a sample. Each resample must be the same size as the original sample (400), and it must be drawn **with** replacement. Without replacement, every resample would contain the same 400 movies as `my_sample` and would always have the same mean.

<average>87</average>

**Answer (z):** `resample.get("rating").mean()`

`resample.get("rating")` selects the `"rating"` column of the resample as a Series, and `.mean()` computes its average. This is the bootstrapped sample mean rating that gets appended to `boot_means` on each iteration.

<average>96</average>

# BEGIN SOLUTION
**Answer:** empirical, statistic

The distribution is **empirical** because it comes from actually running a simulation, 10,000 resamples, and recording the results. A probability distribution would describe the theoretical chances of every possible outcome, not results we observed.

Each value in `boot_means` is a **statistic** because it is a mean computed from a resample, which is data we have. A parameter is a number describing the whole population, like the true mean rating of all Oscar-nominated movies, and there is only one of it, so it wouldn't have a distribution.

# BEGIN SUBPROB

Suppose we use the array `boot_means` to calculate a 90% confidence
interval for the mean rating of Oscar-nominated movies. **Select all**
correct conclusions we can draw about this interval.

[ ] There is a 90% chance that the true mean rating of all Oscar-nominated movies falls within this interval.
[ ] The sample mean rating is within 90% of the true mean rating of all Oscar-nominated movies.
[ ] If we looked at the ratings of many Oscar-nominated movies, about 90% of them would fall within this range.
[ ] None of the above.

# BEGIN SOLUTION
**Answer:** None of the above.

Option 1 is false. The true mean rating of all Oscar-nominated movies is a fixed number, not random, so a specific interval either contains it or doesn't. There's no 90% chance about it. The 90% describes the method: if we repeated the whole process (collect a new sample, bootstrap, build an interval) many times, about 90% of the intervals we made would contain the true mean.

Option 2 is false. A confidence interval doesn't say anything about the sample mean being "within 90%" of the true mean. The confidence level is about how often the interval-building process captures the parameter, not a percentage distance between two numbers.

Option 3 is false. The confidence interval estimates the **mean** rating, not the ratings of individual movies. Individual ratings are much more spread out than sample means, so far fewer than 90% of movies would have ratings inside this interval.

Since none of the first three statements are correct, the answer is "None of the above."

# BEGIN SUBPROB

Suppose both of the following expressions evaluate to `True`.

-   `my_sample.get("rating").mean() == 61.25`

-   `np.std(my_sample.get("rating")) == 15`

What are the left and right endpoints of a 95% CLT-based confidence interval for the mean rating of Oscar-nominated movies?


# BEGIN SOLUTION
**Answer:** left endpoint: 59.75, right endpoint: 62.75

By the Central Limit Theorem, the distribution of the sample mean is roughly normal, centered at the population mean, with a standard deviation of $\frac{\text{population SD}}{\sqrt{\text{sample size}}}$. We don't know the population SD, so we use the sample SD, 15, in its place. Our sample size is 400, so the SD of the sample mean is $\frac{15}{\sqrt{400}} = \frac{15}{20} = 0.75$.

About 95% of the area under a normal curve is within 2 standard deviations of the mean, so a 95% confidence interval goes 2 SDs of the sample mean in each direction from our sample mean: $61.25 \pm 2 \cdot 0.75 = 61.25 \pm 1.5$.

This gives a left endpoint of $61.25 - 1.5 = 59.75$ and a right endpoint of $61.25 + 1.5 = 62.75$.
