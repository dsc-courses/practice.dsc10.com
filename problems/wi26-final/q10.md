# BEGIN PROB

Austin tests whether **mammals** and **birds** have the same proportion of **“fast”** animals (max speed **> 40 mph**). He uses a DataFrame `df` with:

- `kind`: `"Mammal"` or `"Bird"`.
- `is_fast`: `True` if max speed > 40 mph, else `False`.

`df` keeps only rows of `zoo` where `kind` is Mammal or Bird. Assume `df` is representative of all mammals and birds for inference where stated.

# BEGIN SUBPROB

**(a)** **Select all** valid statements of the **null hypothesis** for Austin’s permutation test.

[ ] The distribution of `is_fast` is the same for mammals and birds.
[ ] The proportion of fast animals is the same among mammals and birds.
[ ] The proportion of fast animals among mammals and birds **in `df`** is the same.
[ ] The average maximum speed is the same for mammals and birds.
[ ] There is no association between `kind` and `is_fast`.
[ ] The proportion of fast animals among both groups is 0.5.
[ ] None of the above

# BEGIN SOLUTION

**Answer:** First, second, and fifth — population-level “same distribution / same proportion / no association.” Not “in df only,” not speeds, not 0.5.

Explanation:

Step 1: Austin wants to determine whether mammals and birds have the same proportion of fast animals. An animal is considered fast if its maximum speed is greater than $40$ mph. The null hypothesis states that there is no difference in the proportion of fast animals between the two groups.

Step 2: The first statement, which says that the distribution of is_fast is the same for mammals and birds, is correct. Since is_fast contains Boolean values, having the same distribution means that mammals and birds have the same proportions of True and False values.

Step 3: The second statement is also correct because it directly states that the proportion of fast animals is the same in both groups. The fifth statement is correct because saying there is no association between kind and is_fast means that knowing whether an animal is a mammal or bird does not change its probability of being fast.

Step 4: The third statement is incorrect because it refers specifically to the proportions observed in df. The null hypothesis concerns the underlying populations, and the observed sample proportions may differ due to random sampling. The fourth statement concerns average maximum speeds rather than proportions above $40$ mph, while the sixth incorrectly assumes that the proportion of fast animals must equal $0.5$.

**(c)** Select a **valid test statistic** for this test.

( ) Proportion of fast mammals minus proportion of fast birds (signed).
( ) TVD between distribution of `kind` among fast vs non-fast animals.
( ) Absolute difference between **number** of fast mammals and fast birds.

**Answer:** None of the choices above are valid — for a **two-sided** test on proportions you need a symmetric statistic (e.g. absolute difference in proportions or TVD on `is_fast` by `kind`); signed difference is for one-sided; other options are wrong per exam solution.

Explanation:

Step 1: Austin wants to determine whether mammals and birds have different proportions of fast animals. Since this is a two-sided test, differences in either direction should provide evidence against the null hypothesis.

Step 2: A natural test statistic is the absolute difference between the proportions of fast mammals and fast birds:

`abs(proportion_fast_mammals - proportion_fast_birds)`

Taking the absolute value ensures that a large difference produces a large statistic regardless of which group has the higher proportion.

Step 3: The first option uses the signed difference in proportions without taking the absolute value. This preserves the direction of the difference, so a large negative difference would produce a small statistic rather than a large positive one. It does not directly work with the intended method of calculating a p-value using only the upper tail.

Step 4: The second option compares the distributions of kind among fast and non-fast animals rather than directly comparing the distributions of is_fast among mammals and birds. The third option compares the numbers of fast mammals and birds instead of their proportions. Raw counts can be misleading when the two groups have different sample sizes.

( ) Resample rows of `df` with replacement.
( ) For each row, flip a fair coin for `is_fast`.

Explanation:

Step 2: To simulate data under this hypothesis, we randomly shuffle the "kind" column while keeping the "is_fast" column unchanged. This changes which animals are labeled mammals or birds without changing their fast or non-fast status.

Step 3: Shuffling breaks the original association between kind and is_fast while preserving the total number of mammals, birds, fast animals, and non-fast animals. We then calculate the test statistic using the shuffled data.

Step 4: Repeating this process many times creates a distribution of test statistics under the null hypothesis. We can compare the observed statistic with this simulated distribution to determine whether the difference in proportions is unusually large.

Resampling rows with replacement or separately resampling mammals and birds would not break the original association in the way required for this permutation test.

**(e)** Consider

```py
def stat(tbl):
    props = tbl.groupby("kind").mean().get("is_fast")
    return np.std(props)
```

Using `stat` as the test statistic with 1,000 null simulations, **select all** true statements.

[ ] The empirical distribution is symmetric about 0.
[ ] The distribution has a peak at 0.
[ ] The distribution contains negative values.
[ ] `stat(df)` is a valid observed statistic for this two-sided test.
[ ] Same shuffles \(\Rightarrow\) same \(p\)-value as absolute difference in proportions.
[ ] Same shuffles \(\Rightarrow\) same \(p\)-value as TVD between `is_fast` distributions by `kind`.
[ ] None of the above

# BEGIN SOLUTION

**Answer:** Second, fourth, fifth, sixth — \(\mathrm{std}([p_m, p_b]) = |p_m - p_b|/2\), nonnegative, peak near 0 under null; proportional to abs diff and TVD for binary groups.

Step 1: The expression `tbl.groupby("kind")` separates the animals into mammal and bird groups. Applying `.mean()` calculates the average of each numerical column within each group.

Step 2: Since is_fast is a Boolean column, its mean represents the proportion of True values, or the proportion of fast animals. Therefore, `.get("is_fast")` retrieves a Series containing two values: the proportion of fast mammals and the proportion of fast birds.

Step 3: The expression `np.std(props)` calculates the standard deviation of these two proportions. For two values, the standard deviation equals half their absolute difference:

`np.std(props) = abs(proportion_fast_mammals - proportion_fast_birds) / 2`

Therefore, larger differences between the two proportions produce larger values of stat.

Step 4: The first statement is incorrect because the standard deviation is always nonnegative, so its distribution cannot be symmetric around $0$ with positive and negative values. The third statement is also incorrect because standard deviations cannot be negative.

Step 5: The second statement is correct in the intended conceptual sense because, under the null hypothesis, the proportions of fast mammals and birds should generally be close together. Therefore, the simulated statistics tend to be concentrated near $0$. The fourth statement is correct because the statistic measures differences in either direction, making it appropriate for the two-sided test.

Step 6: The fifth statement is correct because stat is exactly half the absolute difference in proportions. Since every observed and simulated statistic is multiplied by the same positive constant, the comparison between them remains unchanged, producing the same p-value.

Step 7: The sixth statement is also correct because, for a Boolean variable, the TVD between the is_fast distributions of mammals and birds equals the absolute difference between their proportions of fast animals. Therefore, TVD and stat differ only by a constant factor and produce the same p-value when using the same shuffles.

**(f)** Austin wants a **95% CLT** interval for the **proportion of mammals that are fast**, with width **at most 0.1** for **any** true proportion. Minimum number of mammals to sample?


# BEGIN SOLUTION

**Answer:** `400`

For a $95%$ CLT interval for a proportion, the width is approximately $4\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}$. This is largest when $p = 0.5$, giving width $\approx 2/\sqrt{n}$. Setting $2/\sqrt{n} \le 0.1$ gives $n \ge 400$.

Explanation:

Step 1: Austin wants to construct a $95%$ CLT confidence interval for the proportion of mammals that are fast, with a total width of at most $0.1$. Using the DSC 10 approximation, a $95%$ confidence interval extends approximately two standard errors below and above the sample proportion.

Therefore:

`Width = 4 * SD(sample proportion)`

Step 2: According to the square root law, the standard deviation of a sample proportion is:

`SD(sample proportion) = sqrt(p(1-p) / n)`

Here, p is the true proportion of fast mammals and n is the sample size.

Substituting this formula into the width expression gives:

`Width = 4 * sqrt(p(1-p) / n)`

Step 3: The question asks for a sample size that works for any possible true proportion. The expression p(1-p) reaches its maximum value when p = $0.5$.

Therefore, we use p = $0.5$ to calculate the largest possible confidence interval width.

Substituting:

`Width = 4 * sqrt(0.5(0.5) / n)`

`Width = 4 * sqrt(0.25 / n)`

Simplifying:

`Width = 2 / sqrt(n)`

Step 4: Since the width must be at most $0.1$, we solve:

`2 / sqrt(n) <= 0.1`

Multiplying both sides by `sqrt(n)` and dividing by $0.1$ gives:

`sqrt(n) >= 20`

Squaring both sides:

`n >= 400`

Therefore, Austin needs to sample at least $400$ mammals for the approximate $95%$ confidence interval to have a width of at most $0.1$, regardless of the true proportion.
