<!-- Question 8 (X pts) -->

Ray is in charge of ordering ice cream toppings from a supplier. He always orders rainbow sprinkles which are a topping made up of tiny colorful candy pieces. The latest shipment of rainbow sprinkles doesn’t look as colorful as he would like. He takes a sample of 100 sprinkle pieces from the shipment and counts how many pieces appear in each color category.

Distribution of Colors in the Sample

| color | blue | pink | orange | white | green |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **count** | 23 | 17 | 16 | 30 | 14 |

The supplier claims that the colors should be **uniformly distributed**. That is, each sprinkle color is expected to appear in equal proportions in any shipment of their sprinkle mix.

We want to use the distributions of colors to test the following hypotheses:

- **Null Hypothesis:** The colors of the sprinkles shipment are uniformly distributed.
- **Alternative Hypothesis:** The colors of the sprinkles shipment are not uniformly distributed.

# BEGIN SUBPROB

<!-- a) (X pts) -->

Which of the following are appropriate test statistics for this hypothesis test? **Select all that apply.**

[ ] A\. The sum of differences in proportions between a sample’s distribution and the uniform distribution.
[ ] B\. The sum of squared differences in proportions between a sample’s distribution and the uniform distribution.
[ ] C\. The mean of differences in proportions between a sample’s distribution and the uniform distribution.
[ ] D\. The mean of absolute differences in proportions between a sample’s distribution and the uniform distribution.

# BEGIN SOLUTION

**Answer:** B and D.

The proportions in each distribution sum to 1. If we subtract the uniform proportion from the sample proportion for each color and add the differences, the result is always $1 - 1 = 0$. The mean of those differences is therefore also always 0, eliminating A and C.

Squaring the differences (B) or taking their absolute values (D) prevents positive and negative differences from canceling. Both statistics are zero when the distributions match and positive when they differ.

# END SOLUTION

# END SUBPROB

<!-- b) (X pts) -->

Suppose we decide to use total variation distance (TVD) as the test statistic for this hypothesis test. Calculate the TVD between the sample’s distribution and the uniform distribution of colors. **Give your answer as an exact decimal and show your work.**

# BEGIN SOLUTION

**Answer:** $0.13$.

Divide each count by 100 to get the sample proportions $(0.23, 0.17, 0.16, 0.30, 0.14)$. The uniform distribution gives each of the five colors a proportion of $1/5 = 0.20$. Subtract $0.20$ from each sample proportion and take the absolute value. TVD is half the sum of those absolute differences.

$$
\text{TVD} = \frac{0.03 + 0.03 + 0.04 + 0.10 + 0.06}{2} = 0.13.
$$

# END SOLUTION

<!-- c) (X pts) -->

Suppose you run a simulation to generate 1000 TVDs between the uniform distribution of colors and samples of size 100 randomly drawn from that distribution. Your observed statistic is the value calculated in part **(b)**.

Your test produces a p-value of 0.075. Which of the following correctly interprets this result?

[ ] A\. Using the standard p-value cutoff of 0.05, you will reject the null.
[ ] B\. There is a probability of 0.075 that the alternative hypothesis is correct.
[ ] C\. The observed statistic is in the 7.5th percentile of simulated TVDs.
[ ] D\. The observed statistic is in the 92.5th percentile of simulated TVDs.
[ ] E\. If the shipment sample were a random sample from the uniform distribution, getting a TVD greater than or equal to your observed statistic would happen 7.5% of the time.

# BEGIN SOLUTION

**Answer:** D and E.

- **D.** A p-value of $0.075$ means that 75 of the 1000 simulated TVDs were at least as large as the observed TVD. The other 925 were strictly smaller, placing the observed statistic at about the 92.5th percentile.
- **E.** The simulations draw from the uniform distribution, as specified by the null hypothesis. The p-value estimates how often a sample from that distribution gives a TVD at least as large as the one observed.

Since $0.075 > 0.05$, we fail to reject the null hypothesis at the standard cutoff.

# END SOLUTION
