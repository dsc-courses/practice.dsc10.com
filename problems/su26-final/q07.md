Marta developed two new ice cream flavors: choco fig and cherry basil. Parlor staff conducted a survey for several weeks offering customers a sample of either flavor. The more popular flavor will be launched for the summer. The DataFrame `survey` contains customer responses.

Each row includes the `flavor` sampled by a customer and whether the customer `liked` the flavor. The first few rows of `survey` are shown below, though `survey` has many more rows than shown.

|   | flavor | liked |
|---|---|---|
| **0** | cherry basil | True |
| **1** | choco fig | True |
| **2** | cherry basil | False |
| **3** | cherry basil | True |
| **4** | choco fig | False |

We want to use the data in `survey` to test the following hypotheses:

- **Null Hypothesis:** Customers are equally likely to like cherry basil and choco fig.
- **Alternative Hypothesis:** Customers are **more** likely to like cherry basil than choco fig.

The **test statistic** will be the difference between the proportion of customers who liked cherry basil out of those who sampled it and the proportion of customers who liked choco fig out of those who sampled it.

# BEGIN SUBPROB

<!-- a) (X pts) -->

We **cannot** use the **absolute difference** between proportions as the test statistic to test our hypotheses. **In no more than three sentences, explain why.**

# BEGIN SOLUTION

**Answer:** Taking the absolute value turns both $0.20$ (cherry basil leads) and $-0.20$ (choco fig leads) into the same statistic. The alternative predicts a higher like rate for cherry basil, so we need the sign to tell those cases apart.

<!-- c) (X pts) -->

Could the following expression evaluate to `True`? **In no more than three sentences, explain your answer.**

```py
(proportion("cherry basil") + proportion("choco fig")) > 1
```

# BEGIN SOLUTION

**Answer:** Yes.

Each proportion describes a different group of customers. If 50% of cherry basil samplers liked it and 80% of choco fig samplers liked it, their proportions would sum to 1.30, which is greater than 1.

A common reason why students said this was False was because they thought Probability should sum up to 1. If we had asked customers which flavor they preferred out of the two, then the proportions would always add up to 1 (the options would be mutually exclusive). But In our example, people tried each flavor independently and reported if they liked it or not.

<!-- d) (X pts) -->

Complete the implementation of the permutation test below which generates 10,000 simulated values of our test statistic. The variable `p_val` should evaluate to the p-value of the test.

```py
diffs = np.array([])
observed_diff = proportion("cherry basil") - proportion("choco fig")

for i in np.arange(10000):
    shuffled = __(i)__
    with_shuffled = survey.assign(shuffled=shuffled)
    props = __(ii)__.get(__(iii)__)
    diff = props.loc["cherry basil"] - props.loc["choco fig"]
    diffs = np.append(diffs, diff)

p_val = np.count_nonzero(diffs __(iv)__ observed_diff) / 10000
```

# BEGIN SOLUTION

**Answer (i):** `np.random.permutation(survey.get("liked"))`

**Answer (ii):** `with_shuffled.groupby("flavor").mean()`

**Answer (iii):** `"shuffled"`

**Answer (iv):** `>=`

Under the null hypothesis, the chance of liking a flavor is the same in both groups, so the `liked` responses are interchangeable across the groups. Shuffling the responses keeps the number of samplers per flavor and the total number of `True` values fixed while randomly reassigning the responses to customers. And this is a permutation test, `np.random.permutation(survey.get("liked"))` samples without replacement by default.

Within each flavor group, the mean of the shuffled responses gives the proportion of `True` values because `True` counts as 1 and `False` as 0. Subtracting the choco fig proportion from the cherry basil proportion gives one simulated difference.

Larger differences favor the alternative, so the p-value is the fraction of the 10,000 simulated differences that are **at least as large as** `observed_diff`, including ties.
