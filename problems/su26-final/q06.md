Sam bakes The Parlor's sugar cones from scratch. He would like to estimate the proportion of customers that purchase their ice cream in a cone. Recall the `week` DataFrame introduced in the previous question. You will use it to answer the following questions.

# BEGIN SUBPROB

Complete the implementation of the function `cone_ci`. The function returns the endpoints of a 95% CLT-based confidence interval for the proportion of customers who purchased their ice cream in a cone based on the data in `week`.

```py
def cone_ci():
    size = week.shape[0]
    prop = week.get("cone").mean()
    std = np.std(week.get("cone"))
    low = __(i)__
    high = __(ii)__
    return [low, high]
```

Fill in blanks (i) and (ii).

# BEGIN SOLUTION

**Answer (i):** `prop - 2 * std / np.sqrt(size)`

**Answer (ii):** `prop + 2 * std / np.sqrt(size)`

The `"cone"` column is made up of `True`s and `False`s, which behave like 1s and 0s. That means the proportion of cone orders is a **mean**, so we can use the CLT. The distribution of the sample proportion is roughly normal, centered at `prop`, with a standard deviation of `std / np.sqrt(size)`. About 95% of a normal distribution is within 2 standard deviations of the mean, so we go 2 standard deviations in each direction from `prop`.

# BEGIN SUBPROB

Consider the following code.

```py
>>> interval = cone_ci()
>>> interval[1] - interval[0] <= 0.10
True
```

What is the minimum value of `size` in `cone_ci` that would **guarantee** that the code above runs as described? **Give your answer as an int and show your work.**

*Hint*: The standard deviation of a collection of 0s and 1s is $\sqrt{p(1-p)}$, where $p$ is the proportion of 1s.

# BEGIN SOLUTION

**Answer:** 400

The width of the interval is the distance from `low` to `high`:

$$\text{width} = 4 \cdot \frac{\text{std}}{\sqrt{\text{size}}}$$

We don't know the proportion of cone orders ahead of time, so to **guarantee** the width is small enough we use the largest possible standard deviation. $\sqrt{p(1-p)}$ is largest when $p = 0.5$, which gives $\sqrt{0.5 \cdot 0.5} = 0.5$. Then we need:

$$4 \cdot \frac{0.5}{\sqrt{\text{size}}} \leq 0.10$$

$$\frac{2}{\sqrt{\text{size}}} \leq 0.10$$

$$\sqrt{\text{size}} \geq 20$$

$$\text{size} \geq 400$$

So the minimum is 400.

# END SOLUTION

# BEGIN SUBPROB

Sam loves making cones and hopes that at least 90% of the population of customers at The Parlor are purchasing cones. Which pair of hypotheses can we test using a confidence interval generated by `cone_ci`? **Select all that apply.**

[ ] **Null Hypothesis:** The proportion of people that purchase cones is at least 0.90. **Alternative Hypothesis:** The proportion of people that purchase cones is less than 0.90.
[ ] **Null Hypothesis:** The proportion of people that purchase cones is equal to 0.90. **Alternative Hypothesis:** The proportion of people that purchase cones is not equal to 0.90.
[ ] **Null Hypothesis:** The proportion of people that purchase cones is equal to 0.90. **Alternative Hypothesis:** The proportion of people that purchase cones is greater than 0.90.

# BEGIN SOLUTION

**Answer:** Only the second option: **Null Hypothesis:** The proportion of people that purchase cones is equal to 0.90. **Alternative Hypothesis:** The proportion of people that purchase cones is not equal to 0.90.

We can use a confidence interval to test hypotheses of the form "the parameter equals some value" vs. "the parameter does not equal that value". If 0.90 is not in the interval, we reject the null; if it is in the interval, we fail to reject the null.

The alternative hypothesis must be two-sided ("not equal to") because the confidence interval cuts off an equal amount from both tails. It can't tell us which direction the parameter is in. The other two options have one-sided alternatives ("less than" or "greater than"), so we can't test them with a confidence interval. The first option also has a null hypothesis that isn't a single value ("at least 0.90").

Sam uses `cone_ci` to produce a 95% confidence interval with endpoints [0.72, 0.81]. Unfortunately, 0.90 does not fall within this interval. Suppose you updated `cone_ci()` to produce a 68% confidence interval with endpoints $[L, R]$.

Could 0.90 fall within the new interval $[L, R]$? **In no more than three sentences, explain why.**

# BEGIN SOLUTION

**Answer:** No.

Both intervals are centered at the same sample proportion, 0.765. A 68% confidence interval goes out only 1 standard deviation in each direction instead of 2, so it is **narrower** than the 95% interval and fits entirely inside [0.72, 0.81]. Since 0.90 is not in the 95% interval, it can't be in the 68% interval either.
