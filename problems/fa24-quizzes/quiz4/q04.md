# BEGIN PROB

Suppose that in `movies`, the average rating of `"Action"` movies is 7.4
and the average rating of `"Horror"` movies is 7.2. Based on this data,
we decide to test the following hypotheses:

-   **Null**: The ratings of `"Action"` and `"Horror"` movies come from
    the same distribution.

-   **Alternative**: On average, `"Action"` movies have a higher rating
    `"Horror"` movies.

We'll use as our test statistic the mean rating of `"Action"` movies
minus the mean rating of `"Horror"` movies.

# BEGIN SOLUTION
- (a): `"Action"`
- (b): `"Horror"`
- (c): `np.random.permutation(action_horror.get("Genre"))`
- (d): `one_stat(new_df)`
- (e): `diffs >= 0.2`

- **(a), (b):** The test statistic is the mean rating of `"Action"` movies minus the mean rating of `"Horror"` movies. After `groupby("New")`, the genre labels become the index, so we pull out each group's mean with `.loc["Action"]` and `.loc["Horror"]`. The order matters: `"Action"` comes first.
- **(c):** Under the null hypothesis, genre has nothing to do with rating. So we shuffle the `"Genre"` column, which randomly reassigns the genre labels to the ratings. We only shuffle movies from `action_horror`, since those are the only two genres in the test.
- **(d):** We calculate the test statistic on the shuffled DataFrame. `one_stat` groups by `"New"`, the shuffled column, not the original `"Genre"`, which is exactly why we pass it `new_df`.
- **(e):** The observed statistic is $7.4 - 7.2 = 0.2$. The alternative says `"Action"` movies have higher ratings, so large positive values point toward it. The p-value is the proportion of simulated statistics that are **at least as extreme** as the observed one, meaning greater than or equal to 0.2.

Suppose that `p_value` evaluates to $0.14$. Using the standard p-value
cutoff of $0.05$, which of the two hypotheses is better supported by the
data?

( ) Null 
( ) Alternative

# BEGIN SOLUTION
**Answer**: Null

The p-value of 0.14 is larger than the 0.05 cutoff, so we **fail to reject** the null hypothesis. If the null were true, a difference of 0.2 or more would come up about 14% of the time just from random chance. That's not unusual enough to count as evidence against the null. Note that this doesn't **prove** the null is true. It only means the data are consistent with it.

What kind of hypothesis test did we perform in this question?

( ) Standard hypothesis test 
( ) Permutation test

# BEGIN SOLUTION
**Answer**: Permutation test

We were checking whether two samples (`"Action"` ratings and `"Horror"` ratings) come from the same population distribution. We simulated by **shuffling group labels**, which is the defining step of a permutation test. In a standard hypothesis test, we'd simulate new samples from a known population distribution that the null hypothesis spells out (for example, flipping a fair coin with `np.random.multinomial`). Here, the null doesn't give us that distribution, so we shuffle instead.
